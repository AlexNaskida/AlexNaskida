## mission

I work within crypto as a technical, political, and philosophical project.

My focus is the construction and analysis of systems where privacy is not an ornament, security is not an afterthought, and trust is reduced to something that can be examined, constrained, and—where possible—replaced by cryptographic proof.

I research how protocols shape autonomy, ownership, identity, coordination, and power.

The central question is not merely what a system can do.

It is:

> Who controls it, who can observe it, who can alter it, and whether the individual still possesses a credible right to leave?

## research position

Privacy is not the concealment of wrongdoing.

It is the structural condition that allows a person, institution, or protocol to retain an interior boundary against continuous observation, prediction, classification, and control. It is the possibility of acting without converting every action into a permanent instrument of someone else’s intelligence.

A privacy-preserving protocol does more than hide information. It determines what must be revealed, what may remain undisclosed, who can verify a claim, and which forms of authority are technically impossible or prohibitively difficult.

Security is therefore not only the protection of infrastructure.

It is the protection of agency.

---

## the cypherpunk commitment

The cypherpunk tradition understood that freedom cannot depend entirely on institutional restraint.

When observation is cheap, permission is centralized, and exit is weak, autonomy becomes conditional. Cryptography introduces another possibility: systems whose guarantees arise from mathematics, explicit rules, and verifiable computation rather than from the permanent goodwill of an intermediary.

That possibility must be treated with discipline.

Decentralization can become theatre. Privacy can fail through metadata. Protocols can reproduce the same hierarchies they claim to escape.

The work is to identify those failures before they become infrastructure.

## how I think

I look for the hidden authority inside the system.

Who issues the credential?  
Who controls the key?  
Who can freeze access?  
Who can change the rules?  
Who owns the records?  
Who benefits from permanent visibility?  
Who has the ability to refuse?  
Who can leave without permission?

I am interested in the point where philosophy becomes architecture and architecture becomes power.

Code is not neutral once it governs access, identity, value, or speech.

Every protocol is an argument about what people may do to one another.

---

## what I build toward

I am devoted to building and researching systems that preserve:

- private communication
- cryptographic identity
- individual ownership
- permissionless coordination
- verifiable consent
- credible refusal
- meaningful exit

The objective is not to escape reality through technology.

It is to construct technical realities in which autonomy remains possible after scale, institutional pressure, and adversarial incentives arrive.

---

## closing thesis

The future belongs to whoever controls the keys, the rails, the records, the standards, and the authority to deny access.

Cybersecurity protects the boundary.

Cryptography makes the boundary enforceable.

Research reveals where the boundary is being weakened.

Crypto is the field in which those questions become operational.
